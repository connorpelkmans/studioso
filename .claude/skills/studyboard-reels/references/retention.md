# Retention playbook

What's here comes from the platforms' own statements where they exist, and from creators with a track record where they don't. Sources are at the end. Treat third-party percentages as rough; compare your own reels against each other instead.

## What the platforms reward

- **Instagram** (Adam Mosseri, January 2025): the top three ranking signals are **watch time, likes and sends**. Creators should track **average watch time, likes per reach and sends per reach**. Sends matter slightly more for reaching people who don't follow you; likes slightly more for followers. Instagram's own ranking explainer (2023) names "how likely you are to watch a reel **all the way through**" as a Reels signal (completion), and Insights show average watch time in seconds plus **skip rate** (the share who swipe away in the first 3 s). Whether "watch time" is also scored as a percentage hasn't been published; don't claim it.
- **TikTok** (its creative guidance, as quoted by ad platforms): put the hook and the point in the **first 3 seconds**, design for sound off with text on screen, shoot native 9:16.
- **YouTube Shorts**: Studio shows **Viewed vs. Swiped Away** (did the first second work?) and **average percentage viewed** (did the rest hold?). A low "viewed" rate means fix the hook; a good "viewed" rate with a low percentage means fix the pacing or payoff.

So every reel has two jobs: **don't get swiped in second one**, and **be worth sending to a friend**. For a student audience, "sendable" means "this is so us" (relatable pain) or "you need this" (useful before exams).

## The hook: three layers firing at once

In frame 0, three things should say the same thing (Kallaway: visuals are far stronger than speech alone, and the combination of text, motion and speech wins):
1. **Visual**: the problem made visible. A calendar week glowing red. Forty chips flying. Not a logo, not a blank background.
2. **Text**: 3–5 big words that distil the hook. Readable in under a second.
3. **Motion**: "just enough". Something alive (a pulse, a shake, drifting chips), but not so much that the viewer can't tell what the video is about.

(Plus a spoken line or sound when there is one: it should match the text, not add a different idea.)

## The hook script: lean-in, interjection, snapback

Kallaway's three-step structure builds a curiosity loop in which each line makes the viewer need the next:
1. **Context lean-in** (line 1–2): make the topic obvious so the right people self-select in, and give them a reason to lean in: shared pain, a benefit, a surprising fact. Lead with the pain or benefit, never the jargon or the product name ("3 exams in 1 week", not "Crunch Forecast analyses your term").
2. **Scroll-stop interjection**: a contrast word, usually "but" ("but get this", "except", "yet").
3. **Contrarian snapback**: turn the expected direction around. The bigger the surprise, the stronger the snap. The video then has to pay it off, or viewers feel cheated and leave.

Example for Studyboard: "3 exams. 1 week." → "Most people find out the week before." → "What if you saw it coming?" → payoff.

### Desire hook (alternative)
A relatable character + a dream outcome + **minimal conditions**. Any unfair advantage ("my $1,200 planner", "if you have 6 hours a day") makes people dismiss it. Constraint-removing phrases help: "free", "in 10 seconds", "without changing how you study".
- About me: "I stopped pulling all-nighters by doing one thing in September." (Only for real creators telling their real story; Studyboard itself must not invent testimonials.)
- Can you: "Can you plan exam week in 10 seconds?"
- If I: "If I had 3 exams next week, here's what I'd do tonight."

### Rules that come out of this
- **Staccato.** The shortest sentences are in the hook; lines get longer later.
- **Speed to value**: the first real "hit" (the fix, the satisfying moment) by about second 4. Don't save the best for the end: anything after people leave might as well be a black screen.
- **No unfamiliar words in the hook.** People bounce when they feel lost. Name the student situation, not the feature.
- **Anchor to what they know**: exam week, "hell week", Canvas, finals, 11:59 PM, the group chat.

**The first 3 seconds, as a recipe** (each step's evidence is in `marketing/reels/HOOK-EVIDENCE.md`) (Connor's review: B/C still too calm; D/E in `marketing/reels/2026-11-20-types-of-students/`):
1. **0.0 s:** an extreme close-up of the emotion (surprise or happiness are found fastest in attention studies; fear is unreliable) (a face filling the frame), a high-contrast mood cue (a pulsing red glow; note that colour changes alone don't capture attention, but *things appearing* do), a hard impact sound, and a 3–5 word gasp line in huge type ("The final is **cumulative**?!"). Already moving at frame 0.
2. **~1.0 s:** a snap-out reveal of the whole situation, with a second hit as the evidence slams in (a stamp: "CUMULATIVE!", "0% DONE").
3. **~1.6 s:** a *specific*, closeable open loop ("Every friend group has all 4. **#4** is the worst 👇"). Information-gap theory says curiosity needs a specific question the viewer can close by watching; "has one of these" is too vague.
4. **~3 s:** the content starts; the music switches from dramatic to playful (contrast is the joke).

**Never open on a title card.** Our first live post opened on "A field guide / Every friend group during finals week" with the cast idling. Insights showed the hook didn't hold, because no situation landed before the first swipe at 2.7 s. Open cold on the most relatable or funny moment (the character mid-reaction, its props already on screen) with the hook line in the eye-line, and let the list or series structure reveal itself after.

Weak hooks: the app's name, "Introducing…", a logo, "Hey guys", a question with an obvious no ("Do you like being stressed?"), a feature name.

## Structure for 8–15 s

1. **0–1.5 s Hook** (lean-in), already on screen at frame 0.
2. **1.5–3.5 s Interjection + snapback**: one or two staccato text beats.
3. **3.5–9 s Payoff**: the fix in 2–3 visible beats, each ≤ 2 s. The satisfying moment (the red week cooling, the chaos snapping into a plan) is the reward; make it the most beautiful motion in the reel.
4. **9–12 s Proof and CTA**: one line, the URL, ~1.5 s.
5. **Loop**: the last frame flows into frame 0 (same background, same colours), so a rewatch is seamless and raises % watched.

## Pacing and craft

- Something changes every 1.2–2 s: a new text beat, an element landing, a camera-style push-in.
- Text beats: ≤ 7 words, ≥ 80 px. Give viewers ~0.3 s per word plus 0.5 s.
- Ease-out for entrances, slight overshoot for things landing. Linear motion looks cheap.
- Companions reacting (a hop, a one-line bubble) are good mid-reel pattern interrupts and give the brand a personality.
- Captions for speech: 2–5 words at a time, timed to the words.
- Sound: rendered silent; add trending or library audio in the app. A real voice usually beats music for trust.

## What works on StudyTok specifically

- **Doesn't look like an ad.** The study apps that grew on TikTok (Jenni AI, Coconote, Knowunity, FocusTree, per agency breakdowns) mostly posted things that look like other StudyTok videos: a student helping students, POV pain points, "study with me", exam-comeback stories. Polished brand promos are the exception.
- **Lead with student stress** (exams, deadlines, the night before) and use the community's tags: #studytok, #studyhacks, #finals, course-specific tags.
- **Text-overlay hook + background track + app demo** is the simplest format that reportedly worked for a focus app; it's what motion-graphic reels from this skill are.
- **Volume and testing**: the winners tested many formats and hooks across several accounts. One reel proves nothing; three hook variants of the same body teach you something.

**2026-11-15** (Instagram and TikTok, North American students, last 30 days):
- The biggest outliers in the student niche were **pure relatability, not demos**: one block of text over a static desk or laptop shot, dramatic or contrasting music, looped. @ale.guides got 1.6M views with 89 followers (587x their median); @rabiya_unfiltered_ got 4.3M with "Not a single weak subject (I'm cooked this semester)". Reach isn't gated by follower count.
- **Our exact theme is validated**: @rufus.creates got 847K views (42x their median) with "How all of the midterm exams genuinely line up the EXACT SAME week", using an ominous trending audio and a fast montage, looped.
- **November is its own theme**: "they k!ll college students in november", "POV: you open Canvas after Halloweekend", "trying to lock in during the last 4 weeks of the semester".
- **Audio carries these**: music-only mixes; dramatic, operatic or ominous tracks contrasting with mundane visuals.
- **App promos that worked** used a creator reaction or comparison hook ("now I understand why orgo students stopped using ChatGPT"), then a quick screen demo. "Comment X and I'll DM you the link" CTAs are common in big posts, but platforms say they demote engagement bait, so prefer genuine questions.

**Breakdown of the top same-week outlier** (@rufus.creates, 847K views, 42x median; `vidiq_watch_shortform_content`, 2026-11-15):
- 7.2 s total. **Frame 0 is a real person** at a desk (front camera, messy room). No logo, no CTA, nothing branded.
- **One caption for the whole video**: white bold sans with a black outline, centred in the lower-middle, never changing.
- **First cut at ~1.9 s, 4 cuts total** (a shot every 1–2 s). The music builds, the beat starts on the first cut (~2 s), and **the heavy drop lands on a cut at ~4 s**.
- Ends on the highest-energy shot and loops straight back to the calm opener. The contrast is what makes it rewatchable.

How we apply it: `marketing/reels/2026-11-16-same-week/` (stock b-roll of a stressed student → the Studyboard calendar as the "proof" shot → a punchline shot, cut on owned music hits).

## Set 3 rules (Connor, Nov 19, 2026)

- **No stock footage of real people.** Animate everything ourselves: phone-UI formats (a lock screen, a group chat, a search bar, notes), the companions, or the real app recorded with `_tools/screenrec.js` (our own product, not stock).
- **The first 2 seconds:** something is already landing at frame 0 (a notification, a message, a half-typed query), with a new onset every 0.3–0.5 s until about 2 s, and the hook sentence readable in about 1.5 s. Phone-UI formats are ideal: everyone reads a notification or a chat bubble instantly, and each new one is an abrupt onset (Yantis & Jonides).
- **Something to react to**: every reel has a built-in reason to comment or send (an honest question, a "that's us" group chat, a punchline). The first native post got zero organic engagement because a demo gives nothing to respond to.
- **The product appears as part of the story**: Studyboard's own notification on the lock screen, a friend's message in the chat, or the real app answering. Never an end card.

## Native, not an ad (default since Nov 18, 2026)

**Why:** Connor: "the music and the way it's set up feels like an ad". The outlier search for the format of student posts that broke out (vidIQ, Sep to Nov 2026, North American students) found the same thing in nearly every one, e.g. @eesa.inoon 208K at 133x their median, @biologyforhealth 945K at 615x, @my_unaestheticlife 1.8M at 370x, @hannurselife 702K at 237x, @studywithaddy1 802K:
- **One shot, static camera, low visual change.** No cuts on beats, no zooms, no slams.
- **One static block of text in the app's own style**, hyper-specific and first-person or "pov:", written lowercase like a student types it. Never animated.
- **Music only, no sound effects** (`sfx_present: false` across the board): a trending pop/hip-hop or chill track. No orchestral hits, thuds or dings.
- **Phone-filmed or a real screen**: the study-app posts that worked showed the app itself on a phone or tablet with an aspirational or relatable line ("pov: you memorized a whole chapter in 30 minutes because ..."), music only, looped.
- 7–12 s, looped.

What made ours read as ads: dramatic orchestral music with hits, sound effects on every beat, huge outlined text slamming in, motion graphics, logo lockups and end cards, cuts every 2 s. Those are the grammar of paid ads.

**Recipe** (`assets/native-template.html`; `marketing/reels/_tools/screenrec.js` records the real app at phone size with the sample semester):
1. **The hook is the sentence**, not the effects. Make it specific and in a student's voice: "you're telling me this app just tells you what you need on the final?? 😭", "pov: your planner warns you which week is going to be rough before it even starts". It's on screen at frame 0, and the screen is already moving (a slow scroll).
2. **The product is just there**: the real app's screen recording, with its own logo in its own header. That's the whole pitch. One small line later at most ("free btw").
3. **Music only**: a calm or trending track, no sound effects. Best of all, post with a trending sound picked in the Instagram app.
4. **Entertainment posts**: one stock shot plus one static text block (`statusBar: false`).
5. **Readable payoff at frame 0; hook ≤ 10 words; 6 s.** From the first native post: 4 s average watch was exactly the reading time of a 14-word hook, and the answer (79.9%) only scrolled into view after people had left. Record the app at `"vw": 360, "dsf": 3` so its UI reads about 20% bigger, and start on the screen that already shows the answer.
6. No end card, no lockup, no "link in bio" on screen. The caption carries the link and the CTA.

## Short format (Nov 18, 2026; superseded by "Native, not an ad" for most posts)

**Why:** reel D's insights showed 5 s average watch on a 17.3 s reel, with views flat after the first hour. The hook held; the body didn't. The body was a slow listicle of small slides that started at 2.5 s, which set an expectation the hook hadn't. Instagram's top-ranked signal is watch time, measured in both seconds and percentage (Mosseri), so a reel people watch 5 s of should *be* about 8 s long. The top student-niche outlier was 7.2 s, a real person at frame 0, one caption, a cut on each beat, then a loop (see "Live outlier patterns").

**Cold open on the climax (Connor, Nov 18: "none of these have a hook that gets you in the first few seconds").** The first cut of this batch opened on a calm person under a two-line explanatory caption, and the payoff came at 6 s. Now shot 1 *is* the payoff: the head hitting the books, the 94%, the 7:58 alarm, the exams slamming into one week. It has 3–5 huge words (120 px, `REEL.hook`), an abrupt onset with an impact sound by 0.3–0.5 s, and a snap zoom (`"zoom": [1.22, 1, 0.4]` in shots.json). Then the story rewinds, and the last shot loops back into the climax. Support: Google's ABCDs ("jump in", start with the action); abrupt onsets capture attention (Yantis & Jonides); an incomplete first line ("Every friend group has a…") opens a specific information gap that the next onset closes 0.3 s later (Loewenstein).

**Recipe** (`assets/short-template.html` + `scripts/make_short.py`; `build_bg.py` with `{"xf": 0.2, ...}`):
1. **8 s** (10 s when a 2 s product fix is added). Four shots of 2.0 s, cut on the hits of `dramatic.wav` from 33.0, so the loop lands on a hit as well.
2. **Real people at frame 0, full-bleed.** Google/Kantar's ABCDs: open with people, framed tight. Nothing small floating in empty space.
3. **One persistent caption** for the whole reel (white Inter with a black outline, native style). Nothing has to be read against the clock, and the hook stays on screen when the viewer decides.
4. **Each shot is worse, funnier or later than the last** (escalation); the last shot pays off the setup ("4 · the worst", "7:58, exam at 8", "94%?!").
5. Optional short **per-shot labels** (≤ 5 words) or **lock-screen timestamps** to carry the joke.
6. **The product is the proof, not an appendix**: the Studyboard calendar or card is one of the four shots (the outlier's "evidence" beat), or a 2 s fix after the punchline that loops back.
7. **Sign-off** = a small icon + "Studyboard" line over the last shot (about 1.2 s); a full end card only if there's time.
8. Cuts dissolve over 0.2 s centred on each hit: native rhythm without harsh cuts.

## Post caption template

```
<A second hook, different from the on-screen one, ≤ 10 words>

<1–2 short lines on what the feature does, in student language>

Free, works in any browser, no account needed. Link in bio.

<A sends prompt or a comment question: "Send this to your study group" / "Which week is your hell week?">

#studytok #studyhacks #<topic> #studentplanner #studyboard
```

**Max 5 hashtags** (Instagram's cap since Dec 2025), specific over popular: hashtags help search and categorisation, not reach (Mosseri). Default set for student humour: #studytok #finalsweek (in season) #collegelife #studentlife #studentmemes; swap in the feature or topic tag when it fits. Put the search words (e.g. "finals week") in the caption's first line too, because Instagram search reads captions. On TikTok, write the words students would search ("exam study plan", "finals week") in the caption, since search drives discovery.

## What to measure (ask the user for these)

- **Instagram:** average watch time, sends per reach, likes per reach (Mosseri's three), plus skip rate if shown.
- **TikTok:** retention curve (where do people drop?), average watch time, shares.
- **Shorts:** viewed vs. swiped away, average percentage viewed.
- Profile visits and link clicks: did it bring people to Studyboard?

Compare reels that differ in one thing (usually the hook) to learn anything.
